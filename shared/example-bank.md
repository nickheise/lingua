# Example bank

A retrieval aid, not an essay. Every entry passes the deletion test: if removing it would not
change any output, it isn't here. Organised by name type (matching `name-types.md`), with
industry and era as columns for cross-cutting, plus dedicated pull-lists for the specific
comparisons the rest of the plugin argues from.

Trademark tiers use the same five values as `name-types.md`: generic · descriptive · suggestive ·
arbitrary · fanciful. This is a heuristic tier assignment, not clearance.

## 2. `rare letters, common sounds`

Rich Barton's advice (high-point Scrabble letters — Z, Q, X, J, K) only works when the rare
*letter* maps to a completely common *sound*. Rare phonemes are a different, costlier thing.

| Name | Letter | Sound it makes | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| Xerox | X | /z/ — completely common | Orthographic distinctiveness (low neighborhood density, ownable spelling) with zero articulatory cost. |
| Kodak | K (×2) | /k/ — completely common | Same mechanism, doubled — visually striking, trivial to say. |
| Coke | K (spelled C+K sound) | /k/ | Rare-*feeling* letter combination, ordinary sound. |
| Zillow | Z | /z/ | Rare letter opens the word, common sound carries it — also aids search-term ownability. |

**The contrast that makes the rule visible:** Xerox vs. Xzrq. Both use rare letters. Xerox
resolves to ordinary English phonotactics (/ˈzɪərɒks/) a four-year-old can say. Xzrq has no legal
vowel nucleus in English phonotactics and cannot be pronounced without inventing sounds — that is
what a rare *phoneme* (not letter) costs you. This is the distinction Barton's own source material
never articulated: he recommends rare letters and separately recommends easy pronunciation without
noticing they can conflict, and his own examples happen to resolve the conflict correctly every
time.

## 3. High generativity — `world, not word`

A name that arrives with a whole nomenclature system, not just a label.

| Name | Vocabulary it supplies | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Scout** | Waypoints, blazes, cairns, routes, contour lines, base camp, field notes — each with an icon already implied | The canonical case (PRD §3.1). Passes `breadth × fidelity` on both halves: a waypoint genuinely *is* a saved position, a blaze genuinely *is* a mark left for whoever comes next. Nothing is being translated. |
| Basecamp | Camp, milestones, check-ins, campfires (group chat) | Moderate breadth, decent fidelity — a project genuinely has a "base" phase and waypoints along the way, though the mapping is looser than Scout's. |
| Slack (the product, not the word) | Channels, threads, huddles — but this vocabulary comes from chat conventions generally, not from the "slack" metaphor itself | Included as a **negative case**: the name does not generate the vocabulary. "Channel" and "thread" would exist under any name. Worth noting when a name gets credit for generativity that actually belongs to the product category. |

Contrast with breadth-without-fidelity failures in §5 — high generativity is necessary but not
sufficient; the vocabulary also has to be honest.

## 4. `read the ending`

Names with lore have a plot attached whether you want it or not. Run the story to the end before
committing.

| Name | The story | How it ends | Live risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Palantír** | A seeing-stone that shows true visions — selectively arranged to deceive whoever looks | Corrupts its user (Denethor, Saruman); Sauron uses it to manipulate everyone who touches one | Critics have used this against the real company Palantir for a decade — "a tool that shows you a manipulated version of the truth" is not a metaphor its critics had to invent. |
| **Icarus** | Given wings of wax and feathers to escape Crete | Flies too close to the sun against explicit warning, the wax melts, he drowns | The compact cautionary case — gorgeous name, means "ignored a warning and died from hubris." Anything that wants "ambition" without "self-destruction" should look elsewhere. |
| **Nike** | Greek goddess of victory, attends the gods in battle | No dark turn — victory is simply what she is, consistently, across the mythology | The rare case where the ending is clean. Explains why it has survived as a brand name for a century without a critic's gift. |
| **Oracle** | The Oracle at Delphi delivers true but deliberately ambiguous prophecies | Croesus asks if he should attack Persia, is told "a great empire will fall," attacks, and it is his own empire that falls — the prophecy was true and still destroyed him | Mostly a clean read for a database/enterprise brand (authoritative source of truth), but the Croesus story is sitting right there for anyone who wants to argue the company's answers can be technically correct and still ruinous. |
| **Janus** | Roman god of doorways, transitions, and duality — two faces looking opposite directions | No death or fall, but the etymological legacy is "two-faced" as a term for duplicity | Real risk for anything in finance or trust-sensitive categories (Janus Henderson, the asset manager, absorbs this reading routinely) — the duality reads as vision/transition in a good frame and as insincerity in a bad one. |
| **Prometheus** | Titan who steals fire from the gods and gives it to humanity | Chained to a rock, an eagle eats his liver daily, it regrows, forever | Reads as "gave humanity a transformative gift" right up until someone reads the ending, at which point it becomes "stole something he wasn't supposed to have and is eternally punished for it" — a live risk for AI-adjacent branding specifically. |

## 6. Coined and respelled

These are not one category — grouping them by surface similarity obscures the real distinction,
which is spellability cost. Vowel-dropping respells (Flickr, Tumblr) pay a real, measurable
spellability tax; true coinages (Kodak, Häagen-Dazs) do not, because there was never a "correctly
spelled" version to deviate from; and several of the most-cited "coined" 2010s names are not
respelled or even coined at all — they're arbitrary or suggestive plain words.

| Name | What actually happened | Spellability cost | Worth it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flickr | Vowel dropped from "Flicker" | Real — "flicker" is the natural spelling from hearing it once; the missing "e" must be memorized | Bought a clear domain in a crowded 2000s Web 2.0 namespace; became the era's signature move, for better and worse (see §7). |
| Tumblr | Vowel dropped from "Tumbler" | Real, same mechanism as Flickr | Same trade — domain availability and a distinctive look, at a permanent "wait, no vowel?" tax that never fully goes away. |
| Figma | Coined, not respelled — no real word "Figm" exists to deviate from | None — there's no correct spelling to get wrong | N/A — this is coined-from-root ("figure"), not a respell, and belongs in that category instead. |
| Notion | Real, correctly-spelled English word ("an idea or belief") | None | N/A — arbitrary-tier plain word, not coined or respelled at all. |
| Stripe | Real, correctly-spelled English word | None | N/A — arbitrary-tier plain word. |
| Slack | Real, correctly-spelled English word/idiom | None | N/A — suggestive-to-arbitrary plain word. |
| Kodak | Invented from nothing | None — no source word existed to misspell | The original template for pure coinage; no respelling penalty because there was no correct form to begin with. |
| Häagen-Dazs | Invented, faux-Scandinavian | Real, but of a different kind — the diaeresis and "-azs" ending are genuinely hard to produce from memory even though there's no "correct" version being deviated from | Bought geographic/quality connotation; the spelling cost is closer to Flickr/Tumblr's than Kodak's because reproduction from hearing it once is genuinely hard. |

**Retrieval note:** when an agent needs "the 2010s respell move," cite Flickr/Tumblr specifically,
not Figma/Notion/Stripe/Slack — the latter four are misfiled under "coined" in casual naming
listicles and the misfiling itself is worth knowing about.

## 7. Breadth without fidelity — the dangerous failure

The most instructive entries in the bank, because they *feel* productive right up until the first
hard question.

| Name (illustrative) | Category | The breadth (arrives in ~10 seconds) | The fidelity failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Garden** (PRD's own case) | Database tool | Seeds, soil, pruning, harvest, compost | "What is pruning?" → "uh, deleting rows I guess." No honest mapping exists; the vocabulary was never load-bearing, just decorative. Breaks on a core surface (data deletion is not peripheral). |
| **Orchard** (illustrative) | CRM | Seasons, ripening, harvest, grafting | "Grafting" would have to mean merging duplicate contacts, and nothing about grafting suggests deduplication — the mapping is reverse-engineered from the metaphor instead of discovered in the product. |
| **Meadow** (illustrative) | Cybersecurity | Wildflowers, pollination, seasons | Breaks immediately on the core surface, not a peripheral one: there is no honest pastoral word for "intrusion," "breach," or "exploit," which are the entire point of a security product. A metaphor that can't name its own core action has failed the breaking-point probe before it starts. |

**Diagnostic:** breadth-without-fidelity entries all pass the ten-second test and all fail the
`breadth × fidelity` test the moment you ask what an *unglamorous* surface (an error state, a
permissions setting, the billing page) is called in that world. Real Scout passes because
"waypoint" survives that question; these fail because their central action doesn't.

## 8. Eras

Naming fashion is real and dated names read as dated. An agent should be able to place a candidate
in time and flag it if it echoes a closed era.

| Era | Signature move | Examples | Read today |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Latinate / coined pharma-style** (1980s–2000s) | Invented word from Latin/Greek-sounding fragments, often ending in -ient, -is, -ent | Acquient, Novartis, Verizon, Accenture | Reads as corporate-consulting or pharma; a startup using this pattern now signals "enterprise vendor," not "startup." |
| **Dot-com respell wave** (2004–2012) | Drop a vowel from a common English word to clear a domain | Flickr, Tumblr, Digg | Reads as *dated* specifically — this is the era with the shortest shelf life; a vowel-dropped name proposed today reads as a 2011 relic before anything else about it is evaluated. |
| **Plain-common-word wave** (2010–present) | A real, ordinary English word, arbitrary or suggestive in its product category, no invented spelling | Stripe, Notion, Slack, Square, Robinhood | Still the dominant mode; ages well because there's no invented spelling to date it — the word itself was never "new." |
| **Where things are now** (2020s) | Two threads running in parallel: (1) continuation of the plain-word wave, now often two real words compounded rather than one (Linear, Anchor, Ramp); (2) literal, function-first names for AI-native products, actively rejecting cleverness (Copilot, Assistant-pattern names) — a swing back toward descriptive that mirrors the feature-profile logic at product scale | Linear, Ramp, Copilot | Function-first naming at the *product* level (not just feature level) is the newest visible pattern — worth flagging as current rather than dated, though it is young enough that "current" and "trend that ages fast" are not yet distinguishable. |

**Retrieval use:** if a candidate is a vowel-dropped respell of a common word, cite this table
directly — it is very likely to read as a 2011 artifact regardless of how the rest of the
evaluation goes.
