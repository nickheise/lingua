# Xzrq

**Profile:** venture / product · **Candidate for:** a developer-tools company · **Stated
rationale from the proposer:** "high-value Scrabble letters, nobody owns it, the domain is
definitely free" · **Run:** hand-written target, Phase 1

**Ergonomics 30 · Brandability: no world, no reader · Practicality: 0 flags**

Read the third verdict again. **Practicality is completely clean** — no trademark conflict, no
domain problem, no SEO collision, no cross-cultural hazard — and the name is still unusable. A
composite score would have averaged a 30, an argued zero and a clean sheet into something in the
sixties and told you nothing. The layers do not average because they are not measuring
commensurable things.

This critique also demonstrates the harder discipline: **a disastrous name still gets an argued
read, not a sneer.** The instinct behind Xzrq is a real and defensible naming principle. It has
been applied to the wrong layer. That is a correctable error, and saying so is more useful than
being funny about it.

---

## Context

Venture profile — the most permissive of the three. It weights **up** distinctiveness,
generativity, verbability and ownability, weights **down** instant descriptiveness, and explicitly
tolerates opacity. If any profile were going to rescue this name, it is this one.

It does not.

**The trap, and it is the most instructive line in this document.** Distinctiveness scores **99**
— the highest score anywhere in these three reference critiques, higher than Scout on any
dimension — and distinctiveness is a dimension the venture profile weights **up**. Re-weight
honestly for the venture profile and Xzrq *improves*.

It improves to unusable. This is the strongest available demonstration that **dimension scores are
not tradeable against each other.** Pronounceability at 8 is not compensable by distinctiveness at
99, because a name that cannot be said cannot be transmitted, and every downstream property —
word of mouth, recall, verbability, the ability to say it on a podcast — is gated behind being
sayable. Some dimensions are floors, not terms in a sum. `ergonomics_score` averages them because
an average is what a mean is; the interpretation layer exists precisely to notice when the average
is lying.

### `rare letters, common sounds` — why Xerox works and Xzrq does not

Both names open with X. Rich Barton's advice to use high-point Scrabble letters (Z, Q, X, J, K)
appears to contradict his own advice to pick a word a four-year-old can say, and he does not
notice the contradiction. His own examples resolve it — and they resolve this case exactly.

| Name | Rare letters | The sound they make | Syllables | Onsets | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|
| **Xerox** | X, X | /z/ and /ks/ — both extremely common English sounds | 2 (ZEER-oks) | /z/, /r/ — both legal | rare letter, common sound ✅ |
| **Kodak** | K, K | /k/ — one of the most common consonants in English | 2 (KOH-dak) | /k/, /d/ — legal | ✅ |
| **Coke** | K | /k/ | 1 | /k/ — legal | ✅ |
| **Zillow** | Z | /z/ | 2 (ZIL-oh) | /z/, /l/ — legal | ✅ |
| **Xzrq** | X, Z, R, Q | /zz/ geminate, then no nucleus at all | **0** | /zzrk/ — illegal | ❌ |

The rule is `rare letters, common sounds`, and it has two halves that must both hold:

1. **The mapping.** Each rare letter must land on a phoneme English speakers already produce
   thousands of times a day. Xerox spends X and buys /z/. Kodak spends K and buys /k/. This is
   where you get orthographic distinctiveness — low neighbourhood density, better search
   ownability — without paying an articulatory cost.
2. **The arrangement.** Common sounds have to be assembled into legal syllables. This half is
   implicit in Barton's examples because every one of them is a sayable word; it becomes explicit
   only when someone tries what Xzrq tries.

Xzrq fails on both readings, and it is worth being precise about which:

- **Letter by letter**, the mapping is not even unreasonable: `x`→/z/ is the Xerox move,
  `z`→/z/ is common, `q`→/k/ is common as a *sound*. Judged one grapheme at a time this looks like
  it obeys the rule.
- **As a name**, it does not, because the rule is about the sound the *name* makes, and there is no
  legal sound this name makes. /zz/ is not an English contrast; /r/ has no vowel to attach to;
  /rk/ cannot close a syllable that never opened. Rare letters landing on rare, sequence-impossible
  sounds is exactly the failure the rule is written to prevent.

**The one-sentence version.** Rare letters buy distinctiveness only when there is a sayable word
underneath to attach it to. Xerox is a *pronounceable two-syllable word* wearing a rare letter.
Xzrq is a rare letter with nothing underneath it.

## Layer 2 — Brandability (argued)

Position plus the strongest counter-argument, as for any other name. The counter-arguments here
are stronger than they look.

### Generativity — `breadth × fidelity`

**Position: zero on both halves, therefore zero.**

`world, not word` — the question is whether the name imports a working vocabulary, and a coined
string with no semantic anchor imports none. Run `six-siblings test` and there is nothing to run
it on: no domain of reference, no adjacent nouns, no icon that is already drawn. Fidelity is not
*failed* so much as *undefined* — there is no metaphor whose internal structure could map onto the
product's, well or badly. `breadth × fidelity` = 0 × undefined.

The practical consequence is a cost that arrives later and is easy to miss now: **every future
feature name is another blank page.** With Scout, naming the next six features is "which bucket
does this live in." With Xzrq, it is six more naming projects. A generative name *is* brand
architecture; a non-generative one is a permanent tax on every downstream naming decision.

**Counter-argument, and it is genuinely strong:** *fully fanciful names with no world at all have
built some of the largest brands in history.* Kodak, Xerox, Häagen-Dazs, Sony, Verizon and Google
were all semantic blanks at birth. Generativity is one criterion among five, not a gate, and its
absence is a cost rather than a disqualification. What those names had was **a hundred million
dollars of advertising to fill the blank**, plus — decisively — the property Xzrq lacks: they were
all effortlessly sayable, so the meaning could be poured in through ordinary speech. An empty
vessel works. An empty vessel with no opening does not. The counter-argument argues against the
generativity finding and leaves the ergonomics finding entirely intact, which is why it does not
rescue the name.

### Cultural familiarity

**Position: zero pre-loaded meaning, therefore maximum education cost.**

*Scout*, *Beacon*, *Anchor*, *Nomad*, *Forge* and *Atlas* arrive already understood — that is
fluency at the semantic level and it is why the cultural commons gets weighted heavily. Xzrq
arrives understood by nobody and cannot even be repeated back, which is the more expensive of the
two failures. A name you cannot pronounce cannot be passed from one person to another, and
person-to-person transmission is the only distribution channel that is free.

No owned-IP exposure — nothing borrowed, no franchise, no Watkins "Copycat" problem. That is a
clean result on a criterion where Palantir and TARDIS would both be flagged. Worth stating,
because an honest critique reports the criteria a bad name passes.

### Lore integrity — `read the ending`

**Position: no lore, and the procedure is still run.**

`read the ending` exists because names with depth have a *plot* attached whether you want it or
not — Palantír is a seeing-stone that shows true visions selectively arranged to deceive and
corrupts whoever uses it, and critics have used that against the company for a decade; Icarus is
the compact version, a gorgeous name that means *flew too close to the sun and drowned*.

There is nothing down there. No myth, no literature, no franchise, no plot to read to the end.
Recorded as a clean pass, and it is a real pass, not a technicality.

**Do not skip the cross-cultural screen on the strength of it.** Strings that look random to an
English eye collide with real words in other languages more often than intuition suggests, and
"it's meaningless" is a hypothesis, not a finding. The screen is run in Layer 3, against
`references/myths-blocklist.md`, and no folk naming myth is repeated here as fact.

### Sound-symbolism fit

**Position: cannot be assessed. Evidence strength: none.**

Sound symbolism compares a name's phonetic personality against the positioning's personality, and
that comparison requires a reliable pronunciation. There is not one: the transcription is a
low-confidence g2p estimate of a string with no nucleus, and two readers would produce two
different noises.

The correct output here is the refusal, not an invention. It would be easy and completely
unfounded to write "the /z/ onset and hard /k/ close read as technical and abrasive." That
sentence would be fabricated evidence about a pronunciation nobody can produce. Sound symbolism is
a **tie-breaker and territory hint, never a scoring rule** — and here it does not even reach
tie-breaker, because there is nothing stable to break the tie with.

### Ownability — `flag, never block`

**Position: maximal ownability. It is the one criterion the name wins outright, and it wins
nothing.**

Neighbourhood density 0, no prior users, no dictionary meaning to compete with, almost certainly a
free domain and free handles everywhere. On the trademark spectrum it is *fanciful* — the
strongest and most defensible tier there is. Under the venture profile, ownability is weighted up.
Xzrq is, by some distance, the most ownable name in these three reference critiques.

`flag, never block`, in the direction that gets forgotten: the rule says ownability has no veto —
and the same rule means **ownability has no rescue.** It cannot sink a candidate and it cannot
save one. Scout is a magnificent name with a badly crowded namespace and remains recommendable.
Xzrq has a pristine namespace and remains unrecommendable. The asymmetry is deliberate: ownability
is a *price*, and a low price on something you cannot use is not a bargain.

**Counter-argument:** for some categories — pharmaceuticals, chemical compounds, ticker symbols,
internal infrastructure — maximal ownability and semantic emptiness genuinely are the brief, and a
sayability cost is accepted knowingly. That is a real exception and it does not apply here: a
developer-tools company lives or dies on developers saying its name to other developers, in
conference talks, in Slack, in podcast episodes, and in `npm install`.

## Recommendation

Not a verdict. An argued recommendation; you decide. Even here.

**Do not ship Xzrq.** The reason is a single dimension, not a general dismissal:
pronounceability 8 with zero syllables. That one number gates word of mouth, recall, verbability,
voice input, and the ability to say the company's name out loud — and no other score compensates,
because it is a floor rather than a term in a sum.

**Keep the instinct. It was correct.** The proposer wanted low neighbourhood density, a clean
search result, an available domain and a fanciful trademark tier. Those are the right things to
want under a venture profile, and Xzrq delivers all four. The error is that it bought them at the
one layer where the venture profile has no tolerance at all.

**The redirect — the same properties, without the cost.** Every one of Barton's own examples is
this move:

1. **Respell a sayable word.** Flickr, Tumblr, Lyft, Fiverr. Ordinary phonology, rare orthography,
   density collapses, the domain frees up.
2. **Spend a rare letter on a common sound.** `rare letters, common sounds` — Zillow, Xerox, Coke,
   Kodak. Take the Z, the X, the K; make it say /z/, /ks/, /k/.
3. **Coin from legal syllables.** Keep the CV skeleton English already accepts and vary the
   phonemes: *Kodak* is /koʊ.dæk/, two perfectly ordinary syllables in an order nobody had used.
4. **Then re-run this rubric.** A coinage built this way typically lands 85+ on pronounceability
   and 90+ on distinctiveness *simultaneously* — which is the combination Xzrq was reaching for
   and missed by trying to buy the second with the first.

**What would change this read:** almost nothing available. A pronunciation guide does not fix it —
if a name needs a pronunciation guide, the guide is the finding. The only version of this name
worth revisiting is one with a vowel in it, and that is a different name.

**What this critique is not:** a claim that the name is silly. It is a claim that it fails
`rare letters, common sounds` on the arrangement half of the rule, that pronounceability is a
floor rather than a tradeable dimension, and that a clean Layer 3 cannot buy back a broken
Layer 1. Those are articulable reasons, and they are the reasons that transfer to the next
candidate.
