# Cross-Cultural Naming: Myths Blocklist

**Standing rule — cannot be missed: never present a naming myth as fact. When uncertain, say
uncertain.** Cross-cultural naming folklore is repeated constantly in naming listicles, and most
of it is false, unfalsifiable, or a real event distorted past recognition. A skill that repeats
folk myths as fact loses credibility instantly.

Every claim below was checked with live web search against Snopes, Language Log, and named
secondary sources rather than recalled from training data. Where a claim could not be resolved
either way, it is filed as **UNVERIFIED**, not asserted in either direction. Do not upgrade an
UNVERIFIED entry to DEBUNKED or VERIFIED without doing the same check.

---

## 1. DEBUNKED — never repeat as fact

Each of these has a specific, named counter-fact, not just "seems dubious."

### Chevy Nova → "no va" (Mexico/Latin America)
The anchor case. **Snopes rates this false.** The Nova sold well in both Mexico and Venezuela.
Pemex, Mexico's state oil company, sold gasoline branded "Nova" at the same time. Linguistically,
"nova" (one word, stress on the first syllable) and "no va" (two words, "it doesn't go") aren't
the confusable pair the story requires — comparable to mishearing "carpet" as "car pet." This is
the flagship example of a marketing-textbook cautionary tale that never checked its own premise.

### Ford Pinto → "Corcel" (Brazil, "small penis" slang)
Language Log traces this one down directly: the Ford Pinto, under any name, was never sold in
Brazil except possibly as a low-volume import. The Ford Corcel was an unrelated vehicle — a joint
Willys-Overland/Renault project Ford inherited when it acquired Willys's Brazilian operation, not
a Pinto renamed to dodge a slang collision.

### Electrolux, "Nothing sucks like an Electrolux" (as an accidental US blunder)
The slogan is real — but the "accident" framing is the myth. It was a deliberate pun, written by
the British agency Cogent Elliot for the UK market, where "sucks" carried no American slang
meaning at the time; the ad won UK advertising awards. The commonly retold version — that
Electrolux naively exported this into the US and it backfired — misrepresents both the intent and
the market it was built for.

### Coca-Cola, "bite the wax tadpole" (China, 1927–28)
There's a real kernel here, which is exactly why it gets distorted. Before Coca-Cola registered
its own official Chinese transliteration in 1928 — 可口可乐 ("kěkǒukělè," roughly "to permit the
mouth to be able to rejoice") — Chinese shopkeepers advertising imported stock in 1927 created
their own off-the-cuff phonetic renderings on hand-painted signs, and some of those informal
strings did read as nonsense or unfortunate ("bite the wax tadpole" among them). That was
grassroots signage the company didn't create and didn't market under — not a corporate naming
blunder. The story survives by collapsing "some shopkeepers improvised something odd for a year"
into "Coca-Cola marketed itself as 'bite the wax tadpole,'" which did not happen.

### Coors, "Turn It Loose" → "suffer from diarrhea" (Spain)
Per David Wilton's *Word Myths: Debunking Linguistic Urban Legends*, there is no evidence Coors
ran the ad campaign this story describes. It circulates constantly in marketing listicles with no
primary source cited by any of them — a pattern worth noticing on its own.

## 2. VERIFIED — safe to cite, with the caveat

Real, documented cases. The caveat that applies to nearly all of them: **the actual commercial
damage was usually far smaller than the retelling implies** — several of these were caught before
they ever reached a customer, which is a case *for* screening, not evidence of a shipped disaster.

### Mitsubishi Pajero → Montero / Shogun
"Pajero" is genuine vulgar Spanish slang. Mitsubishi renamed the vehicle Montero for Spain, the
Americas outside Brazil, and India, and Shogun for the UK. *Caveat:* this is screening working as
intended — a rename made proactively, not a blunder that shipped and was later walked back.

### Honda Fitta → Jazz
"Fitta" is a documented vulgar term in Swedish (and caused similar trouble in Spain and Italy).
Honda's multi-market feedback process caught it before launch; the car ships as "Jazz" in Europe
and "Fit" in North America. *Caveat:* again, caught pre-launch — evidence the screening step
matters, not evidence that a bad name reached the public.

### Vicks → Wick (German-speaking markets)
"Vicks," pronounced with German phonetics, collides with vulgar slang (a near-homophone of
"wichsen"). The brand sells as "Wick" throughout German-speaking markets except Switzerland.
Long-standing, low-drama rename — no dramatic "blunder" narrative attaches to it, which is itself
informative about what a real, quietly-handled collision looks like.

### Nokia Lumia / "lumia" as Spanish slang
Real, but narrower than the controversy implied: per the Real Academia Española, "lumia" is a
dialectal synonym for "prostituta," entering Spanish through Caló (Romani-influenced) usage and
regionally limited within Spain, with little currency in Latin America. *Caveat:* the word is
obscure enough that the controversy stayed contained and did not visibly affect the product line's
trajectory.

### Parker Pen, "embarrass" → "embarazar" (Mexico)
"Embarazar" means "to impregnate," not "to embarrass" — a real false-friend translation error in
proposed ad copy ("won't leak in your pocket and embarrass you" became a claim about pregnancy).
*Caveat:* documented sources indicate this was caught in pre-launch market testing and never
actually ran as a published ad — real error, contained before release.

### KFC, "finger lickin' good" → "eat your fingers off" (China, 1987)
A real, literal mistranslation on KFC's 1987 entry into the Chinese market. *Caveat:* corrected
quickly, and did not meaningfully impede KFC's later dominance in that market — a real error with
a short half-life, not a lasting brand wound.

### Braniff, "Fly in Leather" → "vuela en cuero" / "fly naked" (1987)
Braniff's Spanish-language radio ads rendered "fly in leather" as "vuela en cuero," a near-
homophone of "vuela en cueros" ("fly naked"). Reported contemporaneously (UPI, February 1987),
making this one of the better-sourced entries on this list. *Caveat:* an idiom translated too
literally by omitting "asientos" ("seats") — a real linguistic near-miss, reported as a PR
embarrassment rather than a measured sales collapse.

## 3. UNVERIFIED — do not use in either direction

Widely repeated. Could not be confirmed *or* refuted. Say so; do not round to either side.

### Pepsi, "come alive" → "brings your ancestors back from the dead" (Taiwan/China)
**Snopes lists this claim's status as undetermined.** The retellings themselves are internally
inconsistent — the language responsible is never pinned down the same way twice (Mandarin,
Cantonese, Thai, and even German versions all circulate), the actual campaign line is usually
misquoted (the real tagline was "Come alive! You're in the Pepsi generation," not "come alive
with Pepsi"), and different tellings claim opposite sales effects. That instability is itself
evidence the story is unmoored from a documented event — but it isn't a specific, named
disproof the way the Nova counter-facts are, so it stays in this bucket rather than DEBUNKED.

### Gerber baby food jar showing a baby "means the jar contains babies" (Africa)
Widely cited as a cautionary tale about literal label interpretation. No primary sourcing turned
up for either the original event or a specific market/country. Commentary from fact-checking
sources treats it as likely urban legend, but without the kind of direct counter-evidence (sales
figures, a named contradicting fact) that the DEBUNKED entries have. Treat as unconfirmed, not
false.

### AMC Matador, "killer" connotation blamed for Puerto Rico road deaths
The naming friction itself is referenced in automotive histories — "Matador" (bullfighter/killer)
did create a real branding awkwardness in Puerto Rico that AMC's own market research picked up.
The escalated version of the claim — that the name is blamed for an elevated road death toll — has
no primary source behind it. Treat the connotation as plausible and documented; treat the
causal death-toll claim as unconfirmed.

## 4. The procedure — screen without relying on the folklore

The folklore above is unreliable in both directions, so the screening method has to stand on its
own mechanism rather than on borrowed anecdotes.

1. **Check phonetic neighbors, not translations.** Nearly every *verified* case above (Pajero,
   Fitta, Vicks/wichsen, Nova's own debunked phonetic argument) is a **sound collision** — the
   candidate name, said aloud, lands near an unrelated word in another language — not a semantic
   mistranslation of a slogan. Run the candidate's pronunciation against the phoneme inventory of
   each language in scope and ask "does this sound like a common word with a bad meaning," not
   "what does this mean in translation."
2. **Name which languages were checked, and which weren't.** A critique that says "screened
   internationally" without naming the languages is making an unfalsifiable claim. State the
   actual list — e.g., "checked: Spanish, Mandarin, German, Portuguese. Not checked: Hindi,
   Arabic, Japanese, and every language not listed" — every time.
3. **Prefer flagging uncertainty over asserting a meaning.** Output shape:
   *"Possible collision with [specific word] in [specific language] — unconfirmed, recommend a
   native-speaker check."* Never *"means X in [language]"* stated as settled fact unless the
   claim has the kind of specific, named sourcing the VERIFIED section above required.
4. **Recommend a native-speaker check for anything actually going to market.** This procedure
   finds phonetic near-misses; it cannot give real clearance, the same way the practicality layer
   overall cannot give legal clearance (see [`03-practicality.md`](03-practicality.md)). A script
   or an LLM checking phoneme inventories is not a substitute for a fluent speaker's ear.
5. **Hold the VERIFIED cases to their own standard.** A real collision, per the entries above, is
   a *specific, named word, in a specific, named language*, ideally corroborated by more than one
   independent source. "I have a feeling this might sound off in some language" is not a finding
   — it's the exact shape of claim this file exists to keep out of a critique.
