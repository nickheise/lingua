# Layer 2 — Brandability: the argued layer

**Nothing in this layer gets a score.** Generativity, resonance and distinctiveness are contested
among professionals; a number here is false precision dressed as rigour. This layer outputs
**five positions, each with the strongest available counter-argument attached.**

The counter-argument is not a politeness ritual. It is the deliverable. A position without its
strongest objection is an opinion; a position that has survived its strongest objection is an
argument the reader can act on. **Write the counter-argument you would least like to answer** — if
it is easy to dismiss, you have not found the real one yet.

**Never a verdict.** This layer produces an argued recommendation. The user decides.

---

## The five criteria, in the order they are run

| # | Criterion | Leading words | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Generativity** | `world, not word` · `breadth × fidelity` · `six-siblings test` | Position + counter |
| 2 | **Cultural familiarity** | commons vs. owned IP | Position + counter, split in two |
| 3 | **Lore integrity** | `read the ending` | Findings + severity |
| 4 | **Sound-symbolism fit** | — | Tie-breaker + **evidence-strength label** |
| 5 | **Ownability** | `flag, never block` | Priced risk + counter |

Run all five, every time, even when the answer is "nothing here." A criterion that reports a clean
pass is information; a criterion that was skipped is a hole.

---

## 1 · Generativity — `breadth × fidelity`

**The single most valuable property of a name, and the one no other naming tool measures.**

`world, not word`. The question is never "is this a good word." It is: **does the name arrive with
a vocabulary and an iconography attached?** *Scout* hands over waypoints, blazes, cairns, routes,
contour lines, base camp, field notes — and each arrives with an icon already drawn. That is not a
name, it is a nomenclature system acquired for the price of one word. *Lingua* hands over tongue,
Babel, Rosetta, grammar: thin, academic, and unmappable onto anything you would build.

A generative name **is** brand architecture. Feature naming stops being a blank page and becomes
"which bucket does this live in."

Generativity has two halves and they multiply: `breadth × fidelity`. Either at zero gives zero.

### Step 1 — Breadth: the `six-siblings test`

**Procedure.** Without straining, name six things in the product that this name's world already
has a word for. Write them as a table: sibling · what it names in the product · is the icon
already drawn?

| Result | Reading |
|---|---|
| Six or more, quickly, with icons implied | Exceptional breadth. |
| Four or five, with effort | Workable. |
| Three or fewer, or you had to invent connections | **Dead end.** Say so in five seconds rather than in eighteen months. |

**Do not stop here.** Breadth is the easy half and it is the half that produces false positives.

### Step 2 — Fidelity: the half that decides it

**Does the vocabulary mean the right things?** Does the metaphor's internal structure map onto the
product's actual structure — or is it merely adjacent?

**Breadth without fidelity is the dangerous failure precisely because it feels productive.** Call
a database tool *Garden* and you get seeds, soil, pruning, harvest, compost in ten seconds. It
feels like the naming problem just got solved. Now ask what "pruning" is. If the answer is "uh,
deleting rows I guess," you have not built a naming system — **you have built a decoder ring users
must memorise.**

> **Arbitrary mapping is worse than a plain name, because plain names don't ask anyone to learn
> anything.** A screen called *Settings* costs nothing. A screen called *Greenhouse* costs every
> user one act of memorisation, forever, and returns nothing for it.

**The fidelity test, per sibling.** For each term produced by the `six-siblings test`, ask: *did I
have to justify this mapping, or was it already true?*

| Signal | Verdict |
|---|---|
| The word's ordinary meaning **is** the product concept | Fidelity holds. Nothing is being translated. |
| The mapping needs a sentence of explanation | Weak. Tolerable once or twice, not six times. |
| The mapping needs "you can think of it as…" | **Fails.** That phrase is the tell. |

Scout passes on every term: a waypoint genuinely *is* a saved position you will return to; a route
genuinely *is* a path through material; a blaze genuinely *is* a mark left for whoever comes next.
Nothing is being translated — the metaphor and the product have the same shape. The test is not
"can I justify this mapping," it is **"did I have to."**

### Step 3 — Probe: *find the breaking point*

Every metaphor fails somewhere. You want to know where **in advance**, not in the design review
eleven months from now.

**The reliable probes are the unglamorous surfaces** — the glamorous ones always work, which is
why testing them proves nothing. Run all four:

| Probe | Ask |
|---|---|
| **Error state** | What is "this went wrong" in this world? |
| **Permissions** | Who has access, and what are they called? |
| **Empty state** | What does "nothing here yet" look like? |
| **Billing / invoicing** | What is an invoice in this world? |

Cartography handles "you're lost" beautifully and has nothing whatsoever to say about invoicing.
That is normal and it is survivable.

**The rule that makes this actionable:**

- Breaking on a **peripheral** surface (billing, legal, account settings) is a **seam**. Name that
  screen in plain English and move on.
- Breaking on a **core** surface — the primary object, the main verb, the thing users do every day
  — is a **hole**, and it is a real problem. Report it as the leading finding.

### Step 4 — Probe: *keep the metaphor off the buttons*

**The metaphor names concepts, not UI actions.** Scout can have *waypoints* as a first-class
concept — in the data model, in the docs, as a sidebar heading — while the button that creates one
still says **Save**.

**Metaphor-as-vocabulary aids memory. Metaphor-as-UI-label costs discoverability.** A button
reading "Blaze this passage" is a puzzle. A button reading "Share" that produces a thing *called* a
blaze is a vocabulary.

Check the candidate against this and state which side it is being proposed on. A world that is
only viable if it takes over the button labels is not viable.

### Position and counter-argument

**Write the position** as a judgement about the product of the two halves, not about the name's
charm.

**Counter-arguments worth reaching for** — pick the one that actually bites:

- *The world is doing work the product has not earned.* An acquired vocabulary sets expectations.
  If the world implies exploration and the product is disciplined retrieval, every sibling term is
  a small mismatch and users feel the whimsy as friction. Generativity is only an asset if the
  world you acquired is the world you wanted — and that is a positioning question, not a naming
  one.
- *Non-generative names have built enormous brands.* Kodak, Xerox, Sony, Google were semantic
  blanks. Generativity is one criterion among five, not a gate. Its absence is a **cost** — a
  permanent tax on every downstream naming decision — not a disqualification. Note what those
  names had that filled the blank: enormous distribution, and effortless sayability.
- *The world will be outgrown.* A metaphor sized to today's product can constrain a five-year
  roadmap. Ask what the world says about the product's second act.

### Profile note

Generativity is weighted **very high** under venture and **low** under feature — a feature does
not need to generate a world, because the parent brand already did, and a feature name that
arrives with its own vocabulary competes with its own product. Under the feature profile this
criterion is largely replaced by the **sibling-consistency check**, which is owned by
[`profiles/feature.md`](profiles/feature.md). Load it and run it there. Microsoft Copilot is the
cautionary tale that check exists for; the profile file carries the detail.

## 2 · Cultural familiarity — split in two, because they behave oppositely

Treating "the name references something people know" as one criterion hides the most important
distinction in this layer. **Always classify first, then evaluate.**

### 2a · Cultural commons — **weight heavily**

*Scout, Beacon, Anchor, Nomad, Forge, Atlas.*

Shared cultural property with no rights-holder. Properties:

- **Pre-loaded meaning** — the name arrives already understood. This is fluency at the semantic
  level, and it is the closest thing to a free asset in naming.
- **Zero education cost** — nobody has to be told what a scout does.
- **Trivially spellable** once seen, and usually already in the dictionary, which helps Layer 1.
- **No rights-holder**, so no borrowed equity that can be withdrawn.

**Weight this heavily.** It is routinely undervalued by people who over-index on distinctiveness,
and it is the single strongest reason to prefer a common word over a coinage.

**Counter-argument, and it is always the same one:** commons is available to everyone, which is
exactly why several large organisations already took it. Familiarity and distinctiveness are in
direct tension, and a commons name buys the first by spending the second. The bill arrives in
Layer 3.

### 2b · Owned IP — **flag as a risk requiring justification**

*Palantir, TARDIS, Skynet, Mordor, Gandalf, franchise and film references.*

You are borrowing equity **you do not control**. Properties:

- Walks directly into Watkins' **"Copycat"** deal-breaker.
- **You inherit the whole plot**, not the part you liked — see criterion 3.
- The rights-holder can act, rebrand, be acquired, or become embarrassing, and you are attached.
- Cultural reference points age, and they age faster than companies do.

**This is a flag, not a prohibition.** The output is: *this is owned IP; here is the specific
inherited risk; here is what would justify accepting it.* A justification usually looks like a
deliberate, defensible thematic claim — not "it sounded cool."

**Counter-argument:** Palantir is a multi-billion-dollar company and the reference has arguably
helped it more than it has hurt. Borrowed equity is real equity. The honest framing is that owned
IP raises variance rather than lowering expected value.

## 3 · Lore integrity — `read the ending`

**Names with lore have a *plot*, and the plot comes attached whether you want it or not.**

- **Palantír** — a seeing-stone that shows true visions *selectively arranged to deceive*, and
  corrupts whoever uses it. Critics have used that against the company for a decade.
- **Icarus** — the compact version. Gorgeous name; means *flew too close to the sun and drowned.*

If a name has depth, something is down there. **`read the ending` before committing.**

### The procedure — run it on every name, including the ones with no lore

1. **Identify the referent.** Mythological, literary, historical, scientific, institutional, or
   none. Note that the *dominant* referent for most people is often not the one the proposer had
   in mind — check which one a search returns first.
2. **Run the full story to its conclusion.** Not the connotation, not the vibe — the plot. What
   happens to this figure, object or place at the end?
3. **Report how it ends**, in one sentence, plainly.
4. **Classify the ending**: clean · ironic · actively damaging.
5. **Assign a severity** and say who would notice: an unfriendly journalist, a competitor writing
   comparison copy, a well-read customer, or nobody.
6. **Check the institutional referent too.** `read the ending` is not only about myth. A word
   whose dominant modern referent is an organisation inherits that organisation's recent history,
   and organisations produce plots faster than mythology does.
7. **When there is no lore, say so explicitly.** "Nothing is down there" is a clean pass and it is
   worth recording, because the cost of *skipping* the check is Palantír.

| Ending | Reading |
|---|---|
| Clean | Record and move on. |
| Ironic — the story undercuts the positioning | A talking point handed to critics. Medium. |
| Actively damaging — deception, hubris, collapse, atrocity | High. Say plainly that this will be used against the product. |

**Counter-argument to weigh honestly:** most customers never learn the lore, and the ones who do
are often charmed by the depth rather than repelled by the ending. The real exposure is
concentrated in adversarial readers — journalists, competitors, regulators — which makes lore risk
a function of how much scrutiny the company will attract, not of how many people know the story.

## 4 · Sound-symbolism fit

**A tie-breaker and a territory hint, never a scoring rule.**

This is the criterion that moved out of Layer 1 (see [`01-ergonomics.md`](01-ergonomics.md) §1).
It lives here because it is a **fit** check, not a property of the name: it compares the name's
phonetic personality against the *positioning's* personality, and it is unanswerable without
knowing the positioning. If no positioning is available, say that, and either ask one question or
skip the criterion. **Do not invent a positioning in order to have something to compare against.**

**Expect to be missing the positioning — this skill's own trigger case is a bare name floated
mid-conversation.** `SKILL.md` Step 1 asks for it directly and upfront, batched with the profile
question, precisely so this criterion doesn't sit structurally dead until Layer 2: *"In one or two
adjectives, what should this feel like to use?"* By the time this section runs, use that answer —
do not ask again.

If it went unanswered, or the critique is running unattended (a blind QA pass from
`brand-name-generator`, for instance), report **"not assessable — no positioning available"** and
move on. That is a clean pass, not a hole: this criterion is a tie-breaker, so an unanswered one
changes no recommendation. Say so explicitly rather than leaving a reader to wonder whether the
step was skipped or failed.

**Procedure.**

1. Read the name's phonetic character from the Layer 1 JSON: onset type, vowel quality, coda,
   syllable count, stress shape.
2. State the positioning's intended personality in two or three adjectives. From the positioning
   canvas if one exists; from the user if not.
3. Say whether they agree, clash, or are neutral.
4. **Label the evidence strength. This label is mandatory.**

| Label | Use when |
|---|---|
| **Moderate** | Size and shape symbolism — the bouba/kiki family. Front vowels and small; back vowels and large. Reasonably replicated. |
| **Weak** | Onset clusters or consonant voicing mapped to brand personality traits. Widely believed, thinly evidenced. |
| **None** | The pronunciation itself is uncertain (`confidence: low`), or the claim is pure introspection. |

5. If the label is **none**, say the criterion cannot be assessed and stop. **Writing a
   confident-sounding sentence about a pronunciation nobody can produce is fabricated evidence.**

**Never let this criterion decide anything.** If it is the deciding argument, the argument is too
thin. Its legitimate uses are breaking a genuine tie between two otherwise equal candidates, and
suggesting which *territory* to explore next.

## 5 · Ownability — the counterweight · `flag, never block`

Ownability is trademark exposure, domain and handle reality, SEO competition, and namespace
crowding. The **facts** belong to Layer 3 ([`03-practicality.md`](03-practicality.md)). What
belongs here is the **argument about what those facts cost**, given everything the first four
criteria found.

**Why it sits in this layer at all:** because it is the direct counterweight to criteria 1 and 2.
**Generativity and familiarity both push *against* distinctiveness.** The names with the best
worlds and the most pre-loaded meaning are, necessarily, the names other people already took.
Scout is a perfect world *and* a crowded namespace: Scout Motors, Scout24, Scout APM, the
scouting movement. That is not a coincidence to be regretted; it is the structure of the problem.

### `flag, never block`

**Ownability is reported with a severity note and has no power to sink a candidate.** The context
profile sets the volume; **nothing sets a veto.**

Scout's crowding is *irrelevant* for a personal tool — nobody is competing for attention inside
your own toolbox — and it is *the whole conversation* for a funded venture, because you will be
buying search terms against Volkswagen's budget. Same fact, same severity, opposite weight, and
the profile is what decides.

**`flag, never block` runs in the direction people forget, too: ownability cannot rescue a name
either.** A pristine namespace does not redeem a name that fails Layer 1 or Layer 2. Ownability is
a **price**, and a low price on something unusable is not a bargain.

### Procedure

1. Take the Layer 3 findings as given. Do not re-derive them here.
2. Name the specific competitors for the term, ordered by collision risk — **same category and
   same buyer first**, loud-but-unrelated second, generic-usage third.
3. Convert the risk into a currency: legal exposure, marketing spend, or nothing at all.
4. State the profile's weighting explicitly, in the form *for X this is irrelevant; for Y it is the
   whole conversation.*
5. Attach the counter-argument.

**The standing counter-argument, and it is strong:** crowded namespaces are survivable and are
survived constantly. *Slack* competed with a common English word and a defunct chat protocol.
*Notion*, *Stripe*, *Arc*, *Linear* and *Cursor* are all dictionary words in busy namespaces and
all won their term through **category dominance**, not prior ownership. Search ownership is
downstream of category ownership more often than the reverse. The honest statement of the risk is
therefore rarely "you cannot own this" and usually **"you will pay to own this, and the bill
arrives in marketing spend rather than in legal fees."**

## Writing this layer

- Five criteria, in order, each with a **position** and a **counter-argument**.
- **No scores. No composite. No verdict.** An argued recommendation; the user decides.
- Leading words verbatim: `world, not word`, `breadth × fidelity`, `six-siblings test`,
  `read the ending`, `flag, never block`. Never paraphrased.
- The headline fragment for this layer is a short clause, not a grade — *"exceptional world,
  crowded namespace"*, *"no world, exact fit"*, *"no world, no reader"*. Two properties in tension
  is usually the honest shape.
- When Layer 1 and Layer 3 disagree with each other about distinctiveness, this layer is where the
  disagreement gets adjudicated — and adjudicating means explaining the tension, not averaging it.
